On the Data Science Mixer podcast, I always ask our expert guests the same question for our “Alternative Hypothesis” segment:

> *“What’s one thing that people* ***think*** *is true about data science or about being a data scientist that you have found to be* ***incorrect****?”*

Guests’ responses to this question have been diverse and fascinating. They’re excited to do a little data-science myth-busting, and they offer thoughtful reasons based on experience for pushing back against conventional wisdom.

Here’s a sampling of responses from some of our recent episodes, with more to come. Be sure to check out the full episodes to hear more from these exceptional data science experts! (Update: We’ve got [a second collection](https://towardsdatascience.com/experts-debunk-even-more-data-science-myths-7a6b8c32f717) of “alternative hypotheses” for your reading pleasure now, too!)

# [**Tessa Jones**](https://www.linkedin.com/in/tessa-jones-27246a51/)**, director of data science, Calligo**

I have maybe five different answers because I think there’s so many misconceptions about data science.

One of the main misconceptions is that the main skill of a data scientist is to be able to code machine learning algorithms, as opposed to building machine learning solutions for a business outcome. Those are two different things. And then I’ll go to the other side of the spectrum: I’ve heard people say that a data scientist is somebody who is really good at pulling data in from disparate sources and making cool visuals that make data meaningful to the business stakeholder. I’ve seen data scientists defined in these two end cases, and I feel like it’s more of a merging of the two.

It seems like there’s this overemphasis on the nuance of knowing the packages to run: “Oh, this is the optimal package to use for building an XGBoost model or building a clustering model.” There’s less emphasis on the nuances of when it matters, or why it matters, or the science. … And without that component, you end up building a lot of things that are really cool, but ultimately, they don’t always end up being super useful to the business. … Unfortunately, they’re not always appreciated because the connection between those worlds isn’t being made.

# [**Margot Gerritsen**](https://www.linkedin.com/in/margot-gerritsen-2b01084/)**, Stanford professor and co-founder of** [**Women in Data Science**](https://www.widsconference.org/)

One myth is that to be a successful data scientist, you must have really strong innate ability. Many people believe that if you don’t have a very high level of natural talent, you’re never going to make it. Now, I’m not somebody to dispute that having a high level of natural talent helps. But there is this myth that without it, you cannot be successful. And that’s just wrong because it ignores the growth aspect of people.

The second myth is really, really unfortunate for women, and that is the myth that this sort of innate ability is more common in men than in women. And this is after decades of debunking and showing that this is not true. Still, many people believe it, and many women believe it. If you believe that innate ability is really, really important, and you believe you don’t have what it takes also because you’re a woman — well, that combination is really lethal.

Both of them have been debunked, but they’re still causing a lot of women to give up or not enter the field because they don’t feel they have what it takes. And if you then enter into a culture that is a little different and you feel a bit left out, it’s easy to conclude then you, indeed, don’t have what it takes.

# [**Kristen Werner**](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kristenwerner/)**, director of data science and engineering, Snowflake**

There’s a sense that if you hire a data scientist … oh, man, they’re going to build a model that tells you what to do next. And I get it. People talk about predictive and prescriptive analytics and are upping the ante of buzzwords from ML to AI.

If you hire a data scientist, they’re going to help you solve problems through a partnership, and maybe that requires a model. But no matter what, it always requires taking a step back, and analyzing and interpreting that model — figuring out how it relates to the business, and interacting with the business to get feedback. There are human-observed things that happen that we can’t figure out with math and statistics. There’s still this expectation that there is some magic inherent to data science that there’s not.
