Throughout my life, whenever I have considered pursuing a new interest, I have always sought out magazines on that topic. My history of magazine subscriptions reflects hobbies adopted and abandoned, curiosities followed and fulfilled, and a few enduring pastimes.

Special-interest magazines like my hobby-related subscriptions represent communities of people interested in a topic and allow readers to determine their fit with these communities. Readers are invited to learn more and explore, guided by a (perceived) personal relationship with an expert editorial staff. These magazines provide readers insights into what to buy, how to act, who key personalities are, and what matters within that topical community (Frith 2012). Readers can judge whether their behaviors and attitudes fit the magazine's and community's norms and then decide whether and how to shape themselves to fit community expectations and membership criteria.

When I learned recently about Michel Foucault's concept of a technology of the self, which he traces through Western cultural history, I immediately thought of how media serve similar purposes—some better than others. Special-interest magazines in particular present opportunities for the reader to develop a self through relationship with the magazines. As I will explore in this paper, there are remarkable similarities between aspects of these magazines and the means of self-development Foucault describes. And yet, though I would argue that magazines have served powerfully as a technology of the self for some time, other media that perform the same functions even more effectively may now eclipse the magazine medium. These insights from philosophy may inform our understanding of the changing role of magazines in **[End Page 1]** intriguing ways and expand our thinking about their past, current, and future role in readers' lives.

## Technologies of the Self

Foucault describes "technologies of the self" as the various means we use to transform ourselves. He writes that these "permit individuals to effect by their own means or with the help of others a certain number of operations on their own bodies and souls, thoughts, conduct, and way of being, so as to transform themselves in order to attain a certain state of happiness, purity, wisdom, perfection, or immortality" (1988, 18). We use these technologies on ourselves for "training and modification," in the sense of gaining both skills and attitudes (Foucault 1988, 18). Foucault addresses in depth "the history of how an individual acts *upon himself*" (emphasis added), shifting away from the otherwise strong focus in his work on how external "technology of domination and power" interacts with the individual's self (19).

Foucault begins his recounting of this history in the first two centuries CE with Greco-Roman philosophy. He notes that during this time, the approach to one's self was "to take care of oneself" or "to be concerned with oneself" (Foucault 1988, 19). Taking care of oneself was to seek wisdom and truth, and this was an ongoing effort throughout life (19).

In contemporary times, however, Foucault argues that the precept "know oneself" has superseded the idea of "taking care of oneself." Taking care of oneself can sound like a selfish, solipsistic act: "We are more inclined to see taking care of ourselves as an immorality, as a means of escape from all possible rules" (Foucault 1988, 22). (Today's self-care discourse offers an interesting contrast.) Christian morality points toward renouncing the self, instead; and external laws imposed by governments provide a secular means of establishing moral guidelines. Therefore, it becomes more important to know yourself in order to reject that self (22).

Caring for oneself in its original formulation, Foucault says, was "a widespread activity, a network of obligations and services to the soul" (1988, 27). The soul could only know itself by considering "the divine element," which would reveal "rules to serve as a basis for just behavior and political action" (25). Certain activities were important in this contemplation, including meditation, retreats, and writing.
