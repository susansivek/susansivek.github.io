## *Renee Teate shared her career move into data science publicly through social media and her podcast, Becoming a Data Scientist. Now director of data science at HelioCampus, she offers more advice for advancing data science careers.*

[Susan Currie Sivek, Ph.D.](https://susansivek.medium.com/?source=post_page-----221c5c860e0e-----------------------------------)

Renee Teate has informed, guided and encouraged many aspiring data scientists through publicly sharing her own career journey into data science. She has hosted a [podcast](https://www.becomingadatascientist.com/category/podcast/), Becoming a Data Scientist; has [blogged](https://www.becomingadatascientist.com/) about her learning and job search; and [tweets](https://twitter.com/becomingdatasci) often about this career shift. Renee recently joined us for a special video episode of the [Data Science Mixer](https://community.alteryx.com/t5/Data-Science-Mixer/bg-p/mixer) podcast, aired at the virtual Alteryx [Inspire](https://inspire.alteryx.com/) conference and also available in our podcast feed.

Here are three “principal components” of what Renee shared that will motivate everyone working in data science to continue their learning and career growth.

> *You already have a lot of what you need.*

Things that I was already good at are the things that have really helped me in my role as a data scientist. Being able to work with data, talk to people about the data, work with stakeholders, explain the results of my analysis, talk to people at universities and colleges, talk to people in different teams within the company — that communication side really helped me move into a leadership role and work with the clients. It wasn’t the technical piece that ended up being my strongest element. Those other aspects of having a career working with data, even if it wasn’t in a machine-learning capacity, really enhanced my ability to make myself valuable in my role and to work with a lot of different teams. To summarize: You already have a lot of what you need. So don’t worry too much. You’ll get the technical skills. Those are easier to pick up, I think.

> *Find a transitional role that lets you take advantage of the skills you already have, and grow your skills while on the job.*

Being able to eventually work with a team really helped because I can lean on others for some of the gaps in my knowledge. I can learn techniques from other people. I try to encourage people that are daunted about getting into data science because there’s so much you can learn on the job. There’s so much that no matter how much background work you do, you’re going to learn more in the job anyway. It’s not like you’re ever done learning to become a data scientist, and then you can get a job as one. A lot of people think they have to learn all the topics on their list before they start applying for jobs. But there’s really a huge need for people with the skills that you have now. So if you can find a transitional role that lets you take advantage of the skills you already have, and grow your skills while on the job — that’s really a good set up, if you can get into that kind of role.

> *We have allies on the team that can explain it in their terms and to other people.*

It starts with how you work with the stakeholders throughout the whole project — how you define what the deliverable is, explain what you’re working on and then bring them along with you. So when we do exploratory data analysis at HelioCampus, we have regular meetings with the end users at the institutions. We show them, “Here’s what we’re finding. Does this make sense? Does this go along with what you expected, or is it surprising to you? And if it’s surprising, we need to check to make sure that we’re correct.” By the time we get to the machine learning piece, they know what inputs are going into the model. We talk about the most important features when we generate the scores, and we deliver the scores in context. We’re not just giving a number. It’s not just a black box.

And so when the end users see those results, they understand it already. When we’re doing our final presentation, even if it includes people who weren’t part of the process, we have allies on the team that can explain it in their terms and to other people on their campus what we did, why we made certain choices and why the results came out the way we did. So we already have the buy-in and the understanding before we get to that final presentation.
